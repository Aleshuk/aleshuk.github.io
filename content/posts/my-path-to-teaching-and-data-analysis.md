I graduated from a school with a strong emphasis on language, particularly French. We had seven French classes a week — writing, reading, geography, a little history, literature. After graduating, one thing I was sure of: I didn't want to work in any linguistic field, or in anything where I had to memorize things and write long, precisely worded reports or essays. (As I mentioned in an earlier post, I'm dyslexic.)

## Falling for math almost by accident

So I entered the physics department. The main driving force was that I genuinely loved math — solving equations, wrestling with problems — and I wanted to stay as far from linguistics as I could.

My previous education hadn't given me much depth in math, so I had a limited sense of its beauty, its applications, what it could actually describe. Despite that, I loved it. In my first years of physics I saw more of the intricacy of mathematics and slowly understood how essential it is — over time I came to see that the world is, at bottom, mathematical. One takeaway stuck with me: how poorly we'd been taught in school. No one had ever shown us the range of what math could do. If I'd known, I would have leaned into it much earlier and gone deeper while I still had the chance.

## Wanting to be the teacher I didn't have

That realization sparked something else: a desire to become a teacher — a better one than the ones I'd had — and to spread some of that beauty around. University gave me the chance, first as a master's student and then as a PhD student. I also taught children aged 6 to 12 the basics of physics and programming at a LEGO education studio.

It was an amazing, fulfilling time. Watching a student's eyes light up when something finally clicks, watching their excitement as they understand a little more of how the world works — that made me genuinely happy.

## Full circle

These days, after teaching myself Python and SQL and working as a data analyst and analytics engineer, I volunteer as a teacher again — passing on what I know to students, younger and older, who are trying to change careers. The same fulfillment is there. Preparing lessons deepens my own understanding of the material, and the sharp, unexpected questions students ask keep me thinking critically long after class ends. 🌳
