*Updated: October 3, 2024*

My journey to Burning Man began with a Ptushkin video back in 2019. For various reasons it took until 2024 to actually make it happen — and this turned out to be the best possible year to start with, largely thanks to the weather. It usually rains very little at Black Rock City, which keeps the ground bone dry and produces near-constant dust storms. This year, though, we got three hours of rain right when we arrived, so it took a week for the ground to dry out and start producing dust again. The result was unusually clear skies and landscapes, and we got to see the full beauty of Black Rock City.

## Preparation

We started preparing a few months ahead by finding a camp to join — an amazing group of people called "Red It Be." We learned what we'd need to bring, keeping in mind one of Burning Man's core principles: full self-reliance. We planned sleeping arrangements (tent, mattress, sleeping bag, pillow), food (canned goods, fruit, snacks, ramen), water (about 2.5 L per person per day), transportation (a bike for the Playa), and other essentials (water bottle, sunglasses, dust goggles, a cup with your name/ID, sunscreen). We also thought about clothing — comfortable shoes, fun hats, tutus for Tutu Tuesday, warm layers for cold nights — and lighting for nighttime (lights for the bike and clothes, plus a headlamp for visibility and safety).

## The journey

We flew from Europe to San Francisco, rented a car with friends, and drove to Black Rock City — about 13 hours including the slow crawl through the gate and the queue. We wanted to arrive early to help set up camp, so we left on Friday and got there by evening. Seeing the desert still practically empty, with nothing built yet, was magical.

## Setting up

After pitching our tents and shade structures, we spent Saturday building the common area — the space where we'd relax, talk, eat, and hide from the sun. On Sunday we set up the stage for the "Red It Be" music camp. Watching an empty patch of desert turn into a home for the week was genuinely fulfilling.

## Camps

Black Rock City hosted about 70,000 people this year. The city itself is laid out like a clock face, with streets named for their position — so an address like "4:00 & B" means the corner of the 4 o'clock street and B street. People come to Burning Man as volunteers, as part of a camp, or as an independent ("free") camper. There's also Burning Man org staff, but most of the people running things are volunteers.

Most people come as part of a camp, and every camp brings something to give the city: cooked food, fresh fruit, drinks (lots of drinks), massages, workshops, bike and shoe repair, free outfits, braids, haircuts — almost anything a city can offer, given away for free. Everyone comes there to give, to share, and that scale of collective generosity is something else. We were part of a camp that provided nighttime entertainment — Berlin-style techno — and we were placed on the edge of the city so the music could carry out into the Playa alongside the other music camps.

## Essentials

I expected the constant music to wreck my sleep, but between the sun and the sheer amount of activity, I was tired enough that it just became white noise. The toilets were close to our camp and cleaned daily. We had a shower in camp that I used twice; the rest of the time, wet wipes did the job just fine. We ate in camp about half the time, and for the rest there was always another camp nearby serving coffee, meals, or a drink with some music on the side.

## Outfits

I'm all for self-expression, one of Burning Man's principles. After watching Ptushkin's video and scrolling plenty of Instagram accounts of Burning Man fashion, I expected something like five out of six people to show up in an elaborate, sophisticated outfit. In reality it was closer to one in six — most people were pretty relaxed about what they wore. To be fair, I didn't spend much time where the Instagram-famous crowd hangs out, so maybe I just missed them. When I got back to Berlin and walked through Friedrichshain, I realized a "special Burning Man outfit" is basically a normal Friedrichshain outfit — and the same one-in-six ratio applies there too. Turns out Burning Man isn't all that different from Berlin. ;)

## The experience

Between the video and the research I'd done, my expectations were sky-high — sophisticated art, workshops you couldn't find anywhere else, wildness, incredible performances. Setting the bar that high leaves a lot of room for disappointment. I didn't personally experience the "wildness" side of it (I'm sure others did). What I found instead was a city — a genuine, functioning city in the middle of the desert. What made it special was the kindness: the sheer desire to give, to share art, love, and creativity. Biking around, we'd constantly hear people inviting us in for coffee, pancakes, food, head massages, puzzles, or some workshop or other.

I went to Burning Man hoping for magic, a break from the news, and a completely new kind of experience. What I actually found was Ukraine — my Ukraine. I went to the Three Gentlemen party, drank vyshnivka at the Kyiv camp, and danced with other Ukrainians. I sang and hugged Alina Pash, a Ukrainian singer who performed there. I visited the Misto camp and had real Ukrainian borscht with pampushky. At the Latvian camp we ate deruny — another very Ukrainian dish. Even a "drama-free workshop" I joined expecting some acting exercises turned into me talking about my actual drama: the pain and worry of the ongoing war. I saw Ukrainian flags on bikes and camps everywhere. I spotted a car in blue and yellow, went over assuming it was a Ukrainian camp, and found out it was actually an American camp that supports Ukraine — I was touched, and I cried. That happened more than once with different camps flying those colors. Even people who were openly pro-Trump Republicans offered their support and told me they believed we'd win — I didn't get into the politics of it, but the support itself meant something.

The real highlight of the trip was the Ukrainian art piece *"I Am Fine"* — made from road signs still carrying the marks of bullets, rockets, and shrapnel. I recognized familiar letters, familiar names, and I cried. Other Ukrainians there gave me a hug. One more thing worth mentioning: every so often we'd hear sirens out on the Playa or back at camp, and I kept wondering who was mixing air-raid sirens into their set. Then one night, out on the open Playa among the art cars, I heard a siren loud and clear and realized it was coming from the "I Am Fine" piece itself — synced, it turned out, to a real siren going off in Ukraine, in my head almost certainly Kyiv. Standing in the middle of the desert, completely disconnected from the news, knowing there'd just been a serious strike aimed at Kyiv's power and water infrastructure — panic set in. I understand what the artist was going for, but living it in that moment was rough.

They say the dust reveals who you really are, since the city strips away most social boundaries and lets you just be yourself. I'd assumed I was this energetic, happy person who'd fill the Playa with dancing, meeting people, chasing sunrises and sunsets. Instead, it showed me I'm a lot like that "I Am Fine" piece — I tell myself and everyone else, with a smile, that I'm fine, but look closer and there are wounds that haven't healed. This Burning Man let some of that surface, and it showed me I actually need the gentler stuff — quiet workshops, tranquility, sleep — to keep going.

## The rest of it

Honestly, I preferred mornings at Burning Man — the air is fresh, there are plenty of workshops, the landscapes are beautiful, and the entertainment is lighter and more engaging. I liked biking around, grabbing a drink, making a bit of art (jewelry, calligraphy, an armor "bikini"), throwing on something light and fun, and just riding.

Nights were their own kind of special. Pulling an all-nighter to watch the sunrise was magical. Nighttime at Burning Man is chaos in the best way — everything and everyone lit up, in constant motion, people and art cars moving unpredictably in every direction. Seeing art we'd already explored during the day take on a completely different feel at night was its own kind of magic.

One especially powerful place was the Temple — a quiet space holding the grief of everyone who'd lost someone. I spent time there letting go of some of my own.

The Burning of the Man itself is a spectacle — a little longer than it strictly needs to be, but beautiful all the same: a fire show, fireworks around the Man, and finally the burn itself.

We left right after the Man came down to beat the exit traffic, and made it back to San Francisco in just six hours, which is apparently fast. Next time, though, I want to stay long enough to see the Temple burn too. Everyone gets their own version of the Burn.

There was a lot to learn and a lot to love about this trip. I'm coming back. 🌳
